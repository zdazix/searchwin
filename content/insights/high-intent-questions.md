## What makes a question high-intent

Someone who asks “what is a dental implant” is learning. Someone who asks “best dentist in Austin for implants” is choosing. The decision to buy is already made. The only thing left is who gets the call.

That second kind of question is what we mean by high-intent. A simple test: would the person asking be ready to contact a business this week? If yes, the question is worth a page. If they are still working out whether they have a problem at all, it can wait.

This matters more in AI search than it did in classic search. An AI answer to a choosing question does not hand over ten links. It names a few businesses and says why. Being one of those names is the whole game, and it is decided question by question.

## The five shapes

Almost every high-intent question is a version of one of these.

| Shape | A local business hears | A national business hears |
|---|---|---|
| Best for | “best dentist in Austin for implants” | “best CRM for a 20-person team” |
| Comparison | “implants vs bridges” | “X vs Y” |
| Cost | “how much do dental implants cost” | “how much does X cost per user” |
| Location | “emergency dentist near me open now” | “accounting firm for startups in the UK” |
| Alternatives | “who else does this in Austin” | “alternatives to X” |

Notice what is missing: “what is”, “how does it work”, “history of”. Those questions bring readers. These bring buyers.

## Where to find yours

Start with what you already have. It is more reliable than any tool.

1. **Your inbox and your phone.** Write down the last twenty questions people asked before they booked or bought. The wording they used is the wording they type into AI.
2. **Your sales conversations.** Note every comparison a prospect mentions: “we are also talking to…”, “is this better than…”. Each one is a comparison page waiting to be written.
3. **Your search data.** In Google Search Console, filter queries that contain best, vs, cost, price, near or alternative. These are the choosing questions you already appear for, often far down the page.
4. **The AI answers themselves.** Ask ChatGPT, Gemini and Google’s AI the questions from steps 1 to 3. Look at who is named, which sources are cited and which follow-up questions are suggested. The follow-ups are free research.

Then rank the list twice: by how close the question is to a purchase, and by whether you can honestly be a good answer to it. Start where both are high.

## What to do with each one

One question, one page. A page that tries to answer five questions is quoted for none of them.

Each page needs three things: the answer in its opening lines, proof next to the claim, and a single next step such as a call, a form or a booking. We describe that structure in detail in [Anatomy of an organic conversion page](/blog/insights/organic-conversion-page-anatomy/).

Before you write anything, check where you stand. Ask the question and see whether you are named today. If you are, the job is to move up and to make the page convert. If you are not, the job is to give AI a page it can quote and sources that confirm it.

## The mistake to avoid

The common mistake is to chase volume. Informational articles are easier to rank and bring more visits, so content calendars fill up with them. The visits look good in a report and rarely turn into enquiries.

Work from the bottom up instead. Cover the questions people ask right before they choose, measure the enquiries each page brings, and widen the topic only when those pages are in place.
